# Nine transcripts to read and decide: the blocked rescue passes

**What you are being asked to do.** For each of the nine recordings below, read the
passages quoted and say which of two transcripts should be the published one. No
engineering knowledge is needed. What is needed is someone who knows these sermons and
these speakers and can tell, by reading, which version is the better record of what was
said in the room.

**What the two transcripts are.** The service transcribes each recording once. On some
recordings it decides the first attempt may have skipped something, so it transcribes the
whole thing a second time. It then has to choose which of the two to publish. On these
nine it chose the first attempt, called here the **primary**, and threw the second away.
The second attempt is called the **rescue**. It was discarded automatically, for one
reason only: it came out with fewer words than the primary. Nothing looked at what the
words were.

Both transcripts still exist on disk, which is why this reading is possible.

**What is quoted below.** For each recording, every stretch of **eight or more words in a
row** that one transcript has and the other does not. Nothing is left out and nothing is
summarised: if a run is quoted at all it is quoted in full. Each run carries the time it
was spoken, a sentence of context either side, and, where the other transcript said
something different over the same stretch of audio, that too. Where a run is Scripture
being read aloud, the reference is named.

Thirty-five runs are quoted in total across the nine recordings. One recording,
`ucf20211106b`, has no run of eight words on either side at all, and what it has instead
is described in its place.

**How to answer.** At the end of each recording there is a verdict line with three
options. Tick one. If the difference does not seem to matter
either way, or if you would need to hear the audio to tell, *unclear* is a real answer and
is more useful than a guess.

**Please do not read Appendix B until you have finished.** It holds a machine measurement
of these same nine pairs. The whole reason this document exists is that the measurement
has been wrong often enough that an independent reading is worth more than a confirmation
of it.

## Appendix A: where this material comes from, and what to distrust in it

Both transcripts of all nine recordings were kept on disk by the 0.6.2 corpus run of
7 September 2026 and are in the repository at
`tools/quality_sweep/results/2026-09-07-rc2/rc062/retained/`. Nothing was re-transcribed
to produce this document, and no audio was decoded. The quotations are the transcripts
verbatim, with only whitespace tidied.

A "run" is a stretch of consecutive words that one pass has and the other does not, found
by aligning the two word-for-word and taking every difference of eight words or more.
Runs shorter than eight words are not listed, so a recording with no runs listed still has
small differences, usually in the hundreds.

**The times are approximate and they come from the primary.** The service produced
per-sentence timings only for the transcript it published, which on all nine is the
primary. A run the primary holds is timed directly. A run only the rescue holds is placed
at the point in the primary where the primary skipped over it, which is right to within a
sentence.

**The legacy archive is not an authority.** It is the transcript the service published for
this recording in an earlier year, and it is machine output from four different generations
of the software. It is quoted here only for its word count. Where it and a transcript
disagree, the transcript that reads like a sentence a person actually said is the one to
believe.

Word counts for the primary and the rescue are the service's own counts, which are the
numbers the discard rule compared. The legacy count is a plain word count of the archived
text and may differ by a handful from how the service would count the same text.

archive corroborates. None of them separates the cases. The longest corroborated run in
